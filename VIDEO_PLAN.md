## The narration — this is the part worth reading twice

The form asks *"Was your code partially or fully generated by LLM models?"* and
we are answering that honestly. So the video should not pretend otherwise either.

**A synthetic narrator stating "I built this" would be a strange kind of
dishonesty** — an AI voice claiming human authorship, in a video whose written
disclosure says an AI wrote the code. I do not think that is what we want.

Three options, in order of how they read to a judge:

| option | how it lands |
|---|---|
| **Your own voice** | By far the best. It is your entry, and a human talking about their own work is whatever the opposite of hollow is. |
| **Text on screen, no narration** | Completely acceptable, and honest. The visuals carry it; captions do the talking. Judges watch hundreds of these muted. |
| **Synthetic voice** | Works, but reads as machine-made next to a disclosure that says the code was too. Risks looking like we hid the AI involvement rather than declaring it. |

**My recommendation:** I build the complete video now with **captions only and the
score underneath** — that is a finished, submittable artifact today. Then, if you
can spend ten minutes recording yourself reading the script below, I drop your
voice in over the same visuals. Same video, better entry, no rework.

I have had a synthetic narrator (Windows `David`) ready the whole time. I am not
going to use it unless you tell me to.

## Narration script (if you record it)

Read at a normal pace; it runs about 95 seconds, which leaves room for the film
and the music to breathe.

**[0:30]**
This is QUANTUM NEGATIVE. A text prompt becomes a short film, and every creative
decision in it — the melody, the colour, the structure — is a measurement on a
quantum circuit. The code renders. It never chooses.

**[0:52]**
The prompt is hashed to a seed. That seed becomes an array of two hundred and
fifty-six values, which the qpixl-v1 engine encodes into a quantum circuit,
measures on ibm_fez — real IBM hardware — and hands back.

**[1:14]**
That deviation, between what went in and what came back, is what drives the
piece. And this is the part I find most interesting.

**[1:20]**
We rendered the same prompt twice: once on the simulator, once on the real
device. On the simulator, the round-trip changes those values by about one
percent. On the hardware, by nearly nine percent — eight point six times more.

**[1:38]**
And the difference is not noise we can average away. Raise the shot count and the
simulator converges toward the exact circuit answer. The device cannot. It settles
at an error floor, because that floor is decoherence and gate error — the physics
of the machine. That is the difference between simulating a quantum computer and
using one.

**[2:00]**
Every decision is recorded in a provenance receipt, with the engine that produced
it, the IBM job id, and the QPU seconds. Two hundred and sixty-nine automated
checks verify the physics and the output. Everything is in the repository.
